A child's seemingly random outbursts or learning struggles might have roots in their earliest development, long before they could even walk or talk. The key lies in understanding how the brain wires itself from the ground up.

First, let's look at the brain's "starter software"—primitive reflexes. These are the automatic movements a baby makes, like startling at a loud noise (the Moro reflex) or grasping a finger. They are essential for survival and early development. But they're supposed to fade away as higher brain functions take over. When they stick around, they can run in the background like a glitchy app, contributing to everything from anxiety and hypersensitivity to impulsive behavior and mood swings, even in adults.

Next, think of the brain's two hemispheres like a car's brake and accelerator. The right hemisphere is the brake: it's cautious, sees the big picture, understands social cues, and helps with sustained focus. The left hemisphere is the accelerator: it's logical, detail-oriented, and drives action. In many kids with behavioral challenges like ADHD, the "brake" is underdeveloped. Without enough braking power, the "accelerator" gets stuck on, leading to hyperactivity, obsessive tendencies, and difficulty seeing the bigger picture.

**Your Practice:**
Try a simple "Brain Harmony" exercise with your child. Have them stand up and slowly march in place, but with a twist: as they lift their right knee, have them touch it with their left hand. Then, as they lift their left knee, touch it with their right hand. This cross-body movement encourages the left and right hemispheres to communicate and work together. Do this for just 60 seconds a day as a playful break.

Think of your child's brain not as broken, but as a symphony orchestra where some musicians need a little more practice. The goal isn't to fix an instrument, but to help the entire orchestra find its rhythm and play in beautiful harmony.
